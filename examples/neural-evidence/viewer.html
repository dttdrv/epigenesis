What protects expression after CHE-1 depletion?

Does moving a 12-base binding core reproduce the effect of moving a larger DNA fragment? Compare the published measurements, then inspect what else changed in each construct.

The measured response

Open dots show control; filled dots show auxin. Dots are supplied observations; horizontal lines are group means. All three constructs share the CHE-1 depletion background. No paired changes or confidence intervals are inferred.

Scroll the chart sideways to compare all three constructs.

Retention is the auxin group mean divided by the control group mean. The endpoints are separate observations, not repeated measurements of the same animal.

What changed in the DNA?

The donor sequences reconstruct the intended edits. They are not final-clone sequencing. A larger fragment changes more than its surrounding bases: orientation and replacement geometry also differ.

What information does a model lose?

A deterministic predictor must return the same value for identical inputs. Compare representations of these two constructs to see whether they can even distinguish the observed means.

This is an algebraic check on this finite dataset. It is not a fitted predictor, a test of equal population means or evidence that richer inputs will predict a new experiment.

What this comparison can tell us

Supported by these observations

The fragment-transfer samples retain more mean gcy-22 expression after depletion than the core-transfer samples. The shared strand-normalized core does not distinguish those constructs.

This can inform the next comparison: separate core orientation from the surrounding sequence, and preserve the replacement interval and spacing.

Still unresolved

Surrounding bases, orientation and geometry vary together. These measurements do not isolate a flank-only mechanism, identify a particular cofactor or measure binding affinity.

Matched constructs and independently identified biological replicates would be needed to test those explanations.
